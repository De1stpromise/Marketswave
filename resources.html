Core Strategies

Six foundational approaches that shape how portfolios are constructed, monitored, and adjusted over time.

1 · Foundation

Asset Allocation

Asset allocation is the deliberate split among major asset classes — primarily equities, fixed income, and cash/cash equivalents, with possible allocations to alternatives. Its purpose is to balance expected return against acceptable risk according to the client’s time horizon and objectives. A longer-horizon investor may hold more stocks, while an investor nearing a spending goal may favor bonds and cash. Managers revisit the allocation as a client’s circumstances or market moves change.

2 · Risk Control

Diversification

Firms diversify across asset classes, industries, regions, currencies, security types, and sometimes managers or funds. The goal is to avoid putting too much capital in investments that may react similarly to economic or market stresses. Combining assets with less-than-perfect correlations can help manage risk, though it cannot eliminate loss. Funds of funds, for instance, spread capital across underlying funds and strategies, with managers conducting due diligence and rebalancing.

3 · Active

Active Management

Active managers research economic conditions, companies, valuations, and securities in an effort to produce returns above a benchmark or meet a specialized mandate. Their work can include stock selection, security analysis, tactical allocation changes, and active risk monitoring. Active management can be useful where manager skill or specialized research may matter, but it normally costs more and does not assure outperformance.

4 · Efficient

Passive Management

Passive management aims to mirror a market index or defined market segment rather than choose securities to beat it. Index mutual funds and ETFs commonly hold the components of a benchmark in similar weights, providing diversified exposure with relatively low fees because they demand less ongoing security selection. A firm may use passive funds as a broad, low-cost core and use active strategies selectively around it.

5 · Discipline

Risk Management

Risk management starts with matching investments to the client’s capacity and willingness to bear risk. Firms use research and monitoring, diversification, benchmarks, position or allocation ranges, and periodic rebalancing to prevent unintended concentration or drift from policy targets. Rebalancing is especially important: it restores intended weights after markets move, keeps risk exposure within the manager’s expertise, and can prevent a portfolio from becoming accidentally concentrated in the assets that have recently risen most.

6 · Expansion

Alternative Investments

Alternatives may include private equity, venture capital, real estate, infrastructure, hedge funds, commodities, derivatives, and — in some mandates — cryptocurrency. Firms use them for additional sources of return, diversification, inflation sensitivity, or specialized exposures. Alternatives are generally a complement, not a substitute, for a sound core allocation. Managers weigh their fees, liquidity constraints, valuation complexity, leverage, and downside risk against the intended portfolio benefit.

How It Works

A clear sequence from first conversation to ongoing service — showing both what the client provides and what the firm delivers at each stage.

1

Discovery and Client Fit

We begin with an introductory meeting, call, or digital form that explains whom the service is for, any account minimums, and the scope of advice. We gather your financial objectives, time horizon, income and liquidity needs, existing holdings, tax considerations, and tolerance for investment losses. This ensures the eventual recommendation is connected to your circumstances rather than a prepackaged portfolio.

2

Plan, Investment Policy & Proposal

Client information is translated into a written plan: target return, risk limits, strategic asset allocation, liquidity reserves, relevant constraints, benchmarks, and the recommended investment approach. A fiduciary process formalizes objectives, time horizon, acceptable risk, expected return, asset classes, and an investment policy statement before implementation.

3

Account Opening, Agreements & Funding

Practical setup work is handled separately from investment selection: completing the advisory agreement, confirming discretionary or advisory authority, opening the custodial account, transferring existing accounts or assets, defining beneficiaries where relevant, and funding the account. We make clear who holds custody, who may trade, how long setup normally takes, what documents are needed, and whether the client must approve trades.

4

Build and Implement the Portfolio

The mandate is translated into investments: select asset classes, diversify holdings, choose active managers, passive index funds, individual securities, or alternatives where appropriate, and execute trades. Portfolio construction is treated as risk-aware implementation — not a guarantee of performance. Managers can use securities such as equities, bonds, commodities, and real estate.

5

Monitor, Rebalance & Manage Risk

Management does not stop after investing. The team monitors portfolio performance, allocation drift, market conditions, liquidity, and investment-specific risks, then rebalances according to the agreed policy and review schedule. Rebalancing is intended to keep exposures within agreed risk parameters as market changes alter portfolio weights.

6

Reporting, Reviews & Communication

Ongoing deliverables include secure online access, account statements, performance reporting against relevant benchmarks, tax documents, periodic review meetings, and a process for updating the plan after life events. Responsibilities extend beyond selecting investments to settling transactions, maintaining records, and preparing clear reports for clients.
